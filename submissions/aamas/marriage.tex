\title[A Sigma Above Average]{A Sigma Above Average: The Social Optimum in
Mate Search Is Not an Equilibrium}

\author{Davi B. Costa}
\affiliation{
  \institution{University of S\~{a}o Paulo}
  \city{S\~{a}o Paulo}
  \country{Brazil}}
\email{davi.costa@usp.br}

\begin{abstract}
We study an agent-based model of mate search in which agents hold private,
asymmetric preferences over one another and meet at random, forming couples and
breaking them up as they go. Marriage enters as a commitment device: an agent
stops searching once the affinity with its partner exceeds a threshold
$\Lambda$. Imposed uniformly, the threshold has an interior optimum. A society
that never commits keeps searching but keeps being abandoned; a society that
commits immediately locks in its first acceptable partner; between the two,
aggregate utility is maximised at $\Lambda^\ast = 1.001\sigma$, one standard
deviation above the mean affinity. This yields what sounds like good advice:
do not marry the first person you like and do not hold out for the love of your
life, but marry if you like your partner more than a sigma above average.

Our main result is that no agent would take it. Holding the society at
$\Lambda = \sigma$, a single agent's best threshold is $1.312 \pm 0.007$, worth
$+0.033 \pm 0.003$ against a typical utility of $1.45$. Iterating the best
response settles at a symmetric Nash equilibrium of
$\Lambda_{\mathrm{eq}} = 1.27$, where aggregate utility is $4.2\%$ lower than at
$\Lambda^\ast$. Commitment generates a positive externality --- an agent that
stops searching stops displacing other people's partners --- which agents
choosing alone do not internalise, so they systematically over-search. We show
the gap is robust to the distribution of preferences and to whether being single
is drawn from the same distribution as being coupled, and that it is unevenly
distributed: once agents differ in how much they are liked, the least desirable
gain most from the convention and the most desirable are made worse off by it.
The advice is sound and the equilibrium does not deliver it.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{Social norms; conventions; decentralised matching; search and
stopping; commitment; price of anarchy; agent-based simulation}

\section{Introduction}

Consider the following situation: you are wondering whether to propose marriage
to your partner. There are many aspects of marriage to consider, but you are
mainly concerned with marriage as a \emph{search strategy}. You like your
partner, but you are not sure it is enough. There may be someone else out there
whom you would prefer, and you are unlikely to meet that person if you marry.
Perhaps the best option is to stay open to the chance. But if your relationship
is symmetric in this regard, you may find yourself single because your partner
has moved on. While marriage removes some of the opportunities available to you,
it also protects you from being left. So: is getting married a good search
strategy?

Put that way, the question is about a \emph{commitment threshold}. An agent
searching for a partner in a market with no central clearing house faces a
stopping problem, and marriage is a device for stopping: a level of affinity at
which the agent binds itself to search no further. Call the level $\Lambda$.
This paper studies what value $\Lambda$ should take, and --- the part that turns
out to matter --- whether agents left to choose it would take that value.

To make this precise we propose an agent-based model. A society of $N$ agents
holds private, asymmetric affinities for one another, drawn from a fixed
distribution. At each of $T$ steps, agents are paired at random; a pair becomes
a couple when both strictly prefer the other to their current situation, and
whoever is left behind returns to being single. An agent with a partner it likes
by more than $\Lambda$ is married, drops out of the matching process for good,
and can neither leave nor be left.

As a uniform rule, $\Lambda$ has an interior optimum. A society that never
commits ($\Lambda = \infty$) keeps searching, but its agents keep being
abandoned, and aggregate utility grows slowly. A society that commits at once
($\Lambda \ll 0$) locks in the first acceptable partner and does worse still.
Between the two, aggregate utility is maximised at
$\Lopt = 1.001\sigma$ in the Gaussian model --- one standard deviation above the
mean affinity. Hence the advice: \emph{do not marry the first person you like
and do not search for the love of your life, but get married if you like your
partner more than a sigma above average.}

Our main result is that this is not something a society of self-interested
agents would arrive at. Fix every agent at $\Lambda = \sigma$ and ask what a
single agent should do. Its best threshold is $1.312 \pm 0.007$, and playing it
is worth $+0.033 \pm 0.003$ against a typical utility of $1.45$ --- a $2.3\%$
gain, ten standard errors from zero. Moving everybody there and repeating, the
iteration settles at a symmetric Nash equilibrium
$\Leq = 1.27$, where aggregate utility is $4.2\%$ below its value at $\Lopt$.

The mechanism is an externality. When an agent keeps searching and succeeds, it
does not merely improve its own position: it displaces someone, who returns to
being single and whose loss is not on the searching agent's books. Committing
internalises that cost. An agent choosing its own threshold weighs only the
option value of continued search against its own risk of being left, and so
searches more than is collectively optimal. That the social optimum sits
\emph{below} the equilibrium --- that everyone should be less picky than each
would individually choose --- is the content of the result.

Our contributions:

\begin{itemize}
\item We define a minimal model of decentralised mate search with a commitment
threshold, and locate the welfare-optimal threshold precisely:
$\Lopt = 1.001\sigma$ for Gaussian affinities, $1.228\sigma$ for uniform ones
(Section~\ref{sec:optimum}).
\item We show this optimum is not an equilibrium. A single agent's best response
to it is $1.312\sigma$, and iterated best response converges to
$\Leq = 1.27\sigma$ with a price of anarchy of $1.043$
(Section~\ref{sec:nash}).
\item We show the welfare gain from the convention is unevenly distributed:
once agents differ in desirability, the least liked gain most and the most liked
are made worse off (Section~\ref{sec:who}).
\item We show the optimum is robust to the shape of the affinity distribution
and to decoupling the utility of being single from the utility of being coupled
(Sections~\ref{sec:optimum} and~\ref{sec:robust}), and compare the decentralised
outcome with Gale--Shapley and the utilitarian optimum
(Section~\ref{sec:benchmarks}).
\end{itemize}

All results are reproducible; code and data accompany the paper.


\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

The idea of using formal tools to study marriage is not
new~\cite{10.2307/1831130, 10.2307/1829987, 10.2307/1837421}. Following the
taxonomy of models of the marriage market
in~\cite{doi:10.1146/annurev-economics-012320-121610}, ours is a search model;
see~\cite{10.2307/23045893, 10.1257/002205105775362014,
doi:10.1146/annurev-economics-111809-125046, 10.1257/jel.20150777} for search
theory and~\cite{10.2307/2780247, 10.2307/2951279, Burdett1999LongTermPF,
10.2307/2999430, 10.2307/44955185,
RePEc:bpj:bejmac:v:advances.1:y:2001:i:1:n:5} for search models of marriage.

We differ from this work in two respects. Rather than analysing matching
properties for generic preferences, we assume a distribution over preferences
and specify a concrete meeting process. The motivation is the regime $N \gg T$,
where the society is far larger than the number of people any agent meets.
Compared with work that does not assume completely generic
preferences~\cite{doi:10.1086/689869, 10.1093/restud/rdw041} we impose
\emph{less} structure in this way.

A famous relative is the stable matching problem~\cite{10.2307/2312726}, with a
large literature on its structure and
algorithmics~\cite{knuthstable, gusfield1989stable, roth1992two} and on its use
in market design~\cite{article, NBERw29367, ren2021matching}. In a society of
millions, however, blocking pairs are guaranteed to exist and irrelevant,
because the two agents will never meet. What matters is the process, not its
fixed points; Section~\ref{sec:dynamics} notes that our system has no
equilibrium configuration to converge to at all, which also limits the reach of
dynamic notions of stability~\cite{Multi-period, doval2021dynamically,
Credibility, DAMIANO200534, akbarpour2014dynamic, PEREYRA2013100, Doval2015ATO,
StabilityMulti-period, DynamicNonlinear, mairesse2016stability,
https://doi.org/10.3982/ECTA10675}. A different route to the large-population
limit of a matching problem is taken in~\cite{Dzierzawa_2000}.

The object we study --- a uniform behavioural rule that is collectively good but
individually resisted --- is a social convention, and the question of whether a
convention is self-enforcing is the standard one asked of conventions in
multi-agent systems. Our model is therefore close in spirit to work in
complexity economics~\cite{comecon, 10.5555/547051}, and it illustrates a case
where utility maximisation does not dictate observed behaviour: the dynamics
follow simple behavioural rules, the resulting pattern is complex, and no agent
has the computational power to work out which rule maximises aggregate utility.

\section{The Mate-Search Model}
\label{sec:model}

The model contains $N$ agents $\Ag = \{1, \dots, N\}$ with preferences over one
another described by a utility function~\cite{mas1995microeconomic}, encoded in
the \emph{affinity matrix} $A \in \R^{N \times N}$: $A_{ij}$ is the utility for
agent $i$ of being in a couple with agent $j$. $A$ is not symmetric in general,
since $i$'s feelings for $j$ need not match $j$'s for $i$, and $A_{ij}$ is not
positive in general, as it is common to meet people one would rather be single
than date. We refer to the collection of agents as the \emph{society}.

\subsection{Affinities}
\label{sec:affinities}

In large societies the distribution generating $A$ matters more than its values.
Agent $k$'s preferences are the row $\{A_{kj} : j \neq k\}$; as with many human
traits it is reasonable to take these as normally distributed. One might want
different agents to have different parameters, but note that the $k$-th
\emph{column} $\{A_{jk} : j \neq k\}$ encodes how agent $k$ is liked by society,
and if rows were Gaussians with agent-specific parameters the columns would not
in general be Gaussian. To keep rows and columns consistently Gaussian we take
all off-diagonal entries from one distribution, $A_{kj} \sim \N(\mu, \sigma)$.
We call this the \emph{Gaussian model}, and for comparison also study the
\emph{uniform model}, $A_{ij} \sim U(\mu - 2\sigma, \mu + 2\sigma)$, which has
the same standard deviation but no tails. Section~\ref{sec:who} relaxes
exchangeability and lets agents differ in how much they are liked.

The diagonal $A_{kk}$ is the utility of being single. Its mean is the average
utility of a society in which nobody has a partner, while the mean of the
off-diagonal entries is the average utility if everyone were paired at random;
the relationship between the two encodes how solitude compares with a random
partner. We do not believe these are generically equal, but present our main
results assuming they are and show in Section~\ref{sec:robust} that the
conclusions survive when they are not.

\subsection{Dynamics}
\label{sec:dynamics}

The dynamics proceed in discrete steps $t \in \mathbb{N}$. At each step the
agents still searching are paired at random --- a uniformly drawn perfect
matching $\pi_t$, with one agent sitting out if their number is odd. If agent
$k$ is single at $t$, so $u_t^k = A_{kk}$, and meets $l = \pi_{t+1}(k)$, the two
form a couple if each prefers the other to its current situation:
%
\begin{align}
  u_{t+1}^k = \begin{cases}
    A_{kl}, & \text{if } A_{kl} > u_t^k \text{ and } A_{lk} > u_t^l,\\
    u_t^k, & \text{otherwise}.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:single}
\end{align}
%
If instead $k$ is in a couple with $m$, so $u_t^k = A_{km}$ and
$u_t^m = A_{mk}$, and $k$ meets $l = \pi_{t+1}(k)$ while $m$ meets
$n = \pi_{t+1}(m)$, then
%
\begin{align}
  u_{t+1}^k = \begin{cases}
    A_{kl}, & \text{if } A_{kl} > u_t^k \text{ and } A_{lk} > u_t^l,\\
    A_{kk}, & \text{if } A_{mn} > u_t^m \text{ and } A_{nm} > u_t^n,\\
    u_t^k, & \text{otherwise}.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:couple}
\end{align}
%
The second line is the externality this paper is about: agent $k$ loses its
partner, and its utility drops all the way back to $A_{kk}$, because $m$ found
someone better. Nothing in $m$'s decision accounts for it. All comparisons are
made against utilities as of the start of the step, and the system starts with
every agent single, $u_0^k = A_{kk}$.

Running for $T$ steps gives $\{u_t^k\}$, from which we take the average utility
and its dispersion,
%
\begin{align}
  u_t \equiv \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^N u_t^k,
  \qquad
  \sigma_t \equiv \Big(\tfrac{1}{N}\textstyle\sum_k (u_t^k)^2 - u_t^2\Big)^{1/2}.
\end{align}
%
Figure~\ref{fig:traj} shows the difference between the two levels of
description. Individual trajectories jump up when an agent finds someone better
and crash back to $A_{kk}$ when an agent is left; the average climbs smoothly.

\subsection{Scale Invariance}
\label{sec:scaling}

Equations~\eqref{eq:single} and~\eqref{eq:couple} depend only on inequalities
between entries of $A$, so the dynamics are unchanged under
$A_{ij} \mapsto \sigma A_{ij} + \mu$, and utilities transform the same way:
%
\begin{align}
  \text{if } (\mu = 0, \sigma = 1) \mapsto u_t^k
  \quad\text{then}\quad
  (\mu, \sigma) \mapsto \sigma u_t^k + \mu.
  \label{eq:scaling}
\end{align}
%
We therefore report everything at $\mu = 0$, $\sigma = 1$; read $0$ as $\mu$ and
the vertical axes in units of $\sigma$. This is what makes ``a sigma above
average'' a well-posed statement rather than a numerical coincidence.

\subsection{No Configuration to Converge To}

In large societies the number of people we meet in a lifetime is far smaller
than the society, $T \ll N$, so the limit $T \rightarrow \infty$ is not the
object of interest. It also does not exist in any useful sense. Consider three
agents $A$, $B$, $C$ where $A$ most likes $C$, $B$ most likes $C$, and $C$ most
likes $A$: at every step one is left out, and they remain in an eternal dance.
The system never settles, which is why we study the trajectory of $u_t$ at
finite $T$ rather than a steady state, and why notions of stability defined on
configurations have limited purchase here. The equilibrium of
Section~\ref{sec:nash} is an equilibrium in \emph{thresholds}, not in matchings.

\section{Commitment}
\label{sec:marriage}

We add a \emph{proposal vector} $\vec{\Lambda} \in \R^N$, where $\Lambda_k$ is
how much agent $k$ must like a partner before committing. A couple is
\emph{married} once the affinity clears both thresholds:
%
\begin{align}
  \text{$(k,l)$ is married at $t$}
  \iff
  A_{kl} > \Lambda_k \ \text{ and }\ A_{lk} > \Lambda_l .
  \label{eq:married}
\end{align}
%
Writing $\Mset_t$ for the set of married agents, the matching at $t+1$ excludes
them:
%
\begin{align}
  \pi_{t+1} :
  \Ag \setminus \Mset_t \rightarrow \Ag \setminus \Mset_t .
  \label{eq:marriedmatching}
\end{align}
%
A married agent is never matched again, so it can never be poached and never
poaches: a married couple never breaks, $\Mset_t$ is non-decreasing, and
$\Lambda = \infty$ recovers the model of Section~\ref{sec:model} exactly.

Two readings of $\vec{\Lambda}$ run through the paper. In
Sections~\ref{sec:optimum} and~\ref{sec:robust} it is a \emph{convention}: a
single number $\Lambda$ that every agent follows, characterising a society, and
the question is which value is best for that society. In
Section~\ref{sec:nash} it is a \emph{strategy}: each agent chooses $\Lambda_k$
for itself, and the question is which profile is stable. The gap between the
answers is the paper's result.

As in \eqref{eq:scaling}, results carry to generic $\mu, \sigma$ provided
$\Lambda$ is shifted and rescaled with the affinities, so
$(\mu, \sigma, \sigma\Lambda + \mu) \mapsto \sigma u_t^k + \mu$. We measure
$\Lambda$ in units of $\sigma$ above the mean throughout.

\section{The Social Optimum}
\label{sec:optimum}

Unless stated otherwise, simulations use $N = 10{,}000$ agents, $T = 100$ steps,
$\mu = 0$ and $\sigma = 1$, and quoted errors are standard errors over
independent societies. Where a society with commitment is compared against one
without, both run on the \emph{same} affinity matrix with independent matchings,
so the difference between them is not an artefact of sampling $A$.

\subsection{An Interior Optimum}

Figure~\ref{fig:lcomp} and Table~\ref{tab:lambda} give aggregate utility as a
function of the convention $\Lambda$. Two limits bracket the behaviour. For
$\Lambda$ above every entry of $A$, commitment never triggers and the society is
that of Section~\ref{sec:model}: agents keep searching, and keep being left, and
$u_{100}$ reaches $1.191$. For $\Lambda$ below every entry, every agent marries
the first agent it likes, and $u_{100}$ falls to $0.868$. Marrying the first
person you like is worse than never marrying at all.

Between the extremes the optimum is interior. Resolving it on a grid of spacing
$0.05$ over $24$ societies, and interpolating the peak through the top three
points, gives
%
\begin{align}
  \Lopt =
  \begin{dcases}
    1.001\,\sigma, & \text{Gaussian}, \quad u_{100} = 1.4508 \pm 0.0013,\\
    1.228\,\sigma, & \text{uniform}, \quad\ \, u_{100} = 1.5784 \pm 0.0007.
  \end{dcases}
  \label{eq:optimum}
\end{align}
%
For Gaussian affinities the optimum is one standard deviation above the mean to
three digits. We do not have an argument for why it should be exactly $\sigma$,
and the uniform model shows it is not a universal constant: with no tails the
optimal threshold moves up by nearly a quarter of a sigma. What the two share is
that the optimum is interior, and close enough to $\sigma$ for the rule of thumb
to be a good one in both.

Hence the advice, which we will spend Section~\ref{sec:nash} showing nobody
would follow: \emph{do not marry the first person you like and do not search for
the love of your life, but get married if you like your partner more than a
sigma above average.}

\subsection{Why There Is an Optimum at All}

The shape of Figure~\ref{fig:lcomp} comes from two opposing effects, and it is
worth naming them because Section~\ref{sec:nash} turns on the fact that an
individual agent sees only one of them.

Raising $\Lambda$ keeps an agent in the pool longer, which is good for that
agent: more draws from $p_A$, and the best of many draws beats the best of few.
This is the option value of search. But keeping an agent in the pool also keeps
it available to poach, and every successful poach sends some third agent back to
$A_{kk}$ --- the second line of~\eqref{eq:couple}. Aggregate utility is the sum
over all agents, so it charges for the displacement. Lowering $\Lambda$ removes
agents from the pool, retiring both their option value and their capacity to
displace others.

The optimum is where the two balance. An agent choosing for itself, however,
weighs its own option value against its own risk of being left --- and the
displacement it inflicts on others appears nowhere in its payoff. That
asymmetry is enough to predict the sign of the result that follows: agents
should want a \emph{higher} threshold than is socially optimal.

\subsection{Commitment Also Reduces Inequality}

Figure~\ref{fig:utumt} plots $u_t$ with its dispersion for the convention
$\Lambda = \sigma$ against no commitment. The mean is higher and the variance
\emph{lower}: in the Gaussian model the standard deviation at $t = 100$ falls
from $0.914$ to $0.645$, and in the uniform model from $0.838$ to $0.371$. If
agents value equality of outcomes this is a second benefit on top of the first.
The mechanism is visible in Figure~\ref{fig:finalhist}: the convention imposes a
floor, since every married agent is above $\Lambda$ by construction, and it
drains the mass of long-term singles sitting at their $A_{kk}$.

\subsection{The Price: A Shorter Search}

The gain is bought with something. Figure~\ref{fig:partners} counts the distinct
partners each agent has over the run: without commitment an agent cycles through
$5.15$ partners on average in the Gaussian model, against $4.04$ under the
convention, and in the uniform model $5.00$ against $2.76$. An agent that does
not commit ends up on average with someone it likes less, but may derive a
surplus from having had more relationships --- a surplus the model does not
represent. Conversely, breaking up is generally painful, and that loss is not
represented either, though including it would only add to the case for
commitment. Evidence suggests marriage is beneficial in a number of
respects~\cite{doi:10.2307/2061670}.

\section{The Equilibrium Threshold}
\label{sec:nash}

% >>> CHANGED FROM ORIGINAL. This section is new; the original paper treats
% Lambda as exogenous throughout and never asks whether an agent would choose
% it. Experiment and data: ../../nash-equilibrium/.
Section~\ref{sec:optimum} treats $\Lambda$ as a convention handed to the
society. Nothing so far makes an individual agent want to obey it. This section
asks whether one would.

\subsection{The Question and How We Measure It}

Fix every agent to a common $\Lambda_r$ and consider one agent choosing
$\Lambda'$ for itself. Because the society is exchangeable, ``one agent'' needs
no qualification: there is no special agent to pick. Write
$\Delta(\Lambda' \mid \Lambda_r)$ for the change in that agent's utility at
$t = T$ relative to conforming. The society is at a \emph{symmetric Nash
equilibrium} when
%
\begin{align}
  \Delta(\Lambda' \mid \Leq) \leq 0
  \qquad \text{for all } \Lambda' ,
  \label{eq:nash}
\end{align}
%
that is, when no agent can do better by moving. Since $N$ is large and one agent
does not move the aggregate, this is a population game and~\eqref{eq:nash} is
its mean-field equilibrium condition. To locate it we use \emph{iterated best
response}: find the $\Lambda'$ maximising $\Delta(\cdot \mid \Lambda_r)$, move
the whole society there, and repeat, starting from $\Lambda_0 = \sigma$.

Measuring $\Delta$ requires care, because two pressures work against each other.
A single agent is far too noisy: its utility at $t = T$ has a standard deviation
of order $\sigma$, while the effect sought is a few hundredths of a utile. But
many deviators are no longer one agent --- let a large group deviate and it
begins responding to itself rather than to the society, since a crowd of agents
all holding out is a crowd of agents still in the matching pool, which changes
the pool. We use $200$ deviators per run, $2\%$ of the society, scattered at
random, with two safeguards.

\textbf{Pairing.} A deviator's gain is measured against \emph{the same agent} in
a baseline run on the same affinity matrix with the same stream of matchings, so
$\Delta$ is a mean of per-agent differences rather than a difference of two
population means and the agent's own luck in $A$ cancels. Deviating to the
convention must then come out identically zero, agent by agent, which is a test
rather than a hope.

\textbf{A witness for the environment.} Every sweep also reports what the
non-deviators got, differenced the same way. That number is the environment
moving; if it is not zero, the measurement is not a best response to anything.

Replication is across societies, never across deviators: two deviators in one
run share a matrix and a matching stream, so their gains are correlated. Each
round below uses $256$ independent societies, and every error bar is taken over
them.

Round $0$ answers the question on its own. With the whole society at
$\Lambda = \sigma$, a single agent's best threshold is $1.312 \pm 0.007$, not
$1.000$, and playing it is worth $+0.0327 \pm 0.0034$ against a typical utility
of $1.45$ --- a $2.3\%$ gain, ten standard errors from zero. A lone agent is
better off holding out for a partner it likes rather more than the convention
asks. The sign is the one predicted in Section~\ref{sec:optimum}: agents want to
over-search, because the displacement they cause is not on their books.

The iteration settles at
%
\begin{align}
  \Leq = 1.266 \pm 0.023 ,
  \label{eq:leq}
\end{align}
%
the spread being over the settled rounds. It arrives in a single step --- the
jump at round $0$ --- and everything after is jitter at the measurement noise of
the argmax, because the best response is nearly flat in the convention it
answers. The column that establishes convergence is the last one, not the
trajectory: a fixed point is reached when no agent can do measurably better by
moving, and from round $1$ onward the available gain is statistically zero. The
iterate sitting still is the weaker test; the incentive vanishing is the real
one.

\subsection{What It Costs}

Aggregate utility peaks at round $0$, exactly where Section~\ref{sec:optimum}
says it should, and falls once agents are allowed to choose:
%
\begin{align}
  u_{100}(\Lopt) &= 1.4487 \pm 0.0004, \notag\\
  u_{100}(\Leq)  &= 1.3885, \label{eq:poa}\\
  \text{price of anarchy} &= 1.043 . \notag
\end{align}
%
Letting agents pick their own thresholds costs $0.060$ of utility, or $4.2\%$ of
the aggregate. Every agent is worse off at $\Leq$ than at $\Lopt$ --- this is a
symmetric equilibrium of a symmetric game, so there is no redistribution
hiding inside the average and no agent that gains from the move.

The structure is that of a commons. Each agent searching a little longer is
individually right and collectively wrong, and the society lands at a threshold
$27\%$ above the one that would serve it best. The advice of
Section~\ref{sec:optimum} is sound as advice; what the model says is that it
will not be arrived at by agents optimising alone, which is the usual reason
societies enforce conventions rather than leaving them to individual judgement.

\subsection{Reading the Equilibrium}

Two remarks on what $\Leq$ is and is not.

It is an equilibrium in \emph{thresholds}, not in matchings. The underlying
matching process never settles (Section~\ref{sec:dynamics}); what settles is the
rule agents use to stop. This is why the equilibrium concept here is the
appropriate one even though stable matchings are not.

It is also the equilibrium of the game at horizon $T = 100$. Both $\Lopt$ and
$\Leq$ depend on the horizon, since a society expecting a long search should be
more demanding than one expecting a short one --- visible in
Figure~\ref{fig:lcomp} as the curves crossing. We fix $T = 100$ throughout; the
horizon-dependence of the gap is left for future work.

\section{Robustness}
\label{sec:robust}

\subsection{When Being Single Is a Different Draw}

Section~\ref{sec:affinities} assumed for concreteness that the diagonal and
off-diagonal entries of $A$ come from the same distribution. To check the
conclusion does not depend on it, we set $A_{kk} = 0$ for every agent --- being
single is worth exactly nothing --- and draw $A_{ij} \sim \N(\mu, 1)$ for
$i \neq j$, sweeping $\mu$ with the convention tracking the distribution at
$\Lambda = \mu + \sigma$. Negative $\mu$ means most people are worse than
solitude; positive $\mu$ means almost anyone beats it.

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig06_mu_robustness}
  \caption{Aggregate utility with $A_{kk} = 0$ and $A_{ij} \sim \N(\mu,1)$ for
  $i \neq j$, $N = 10{,}000$. Orange: the convention $\Lambda = \mu + 1$. Blue:
  no commitment.}
  \label{fig:mu}
  \Description{Five panels, one per value of mu, each showing an orange curve at
  or above a blue curve, with the gap widening as mu increases.}
\end{figure*}

Writing $f(\mu) = u_M(\mu) - u(\mu)$ for the gap between the society with the
convention and the society without, Figure~\ref{fig:mu} gives $f(-2) = +0.001$,
$f(-1) = +0.027$, $f(0) = +0.283$, $f(1) = +0.565$, $f(2) = +0.739$: positive
throughout and non-decreasing in $\mu$. The ends of the sweep also cover what
lies outside it. Below $\mu = -2\sigma$ an agent rarely meets anyone it prefers
to solitude and the system behaves as at $\mu = -2$; above $\mu = 2\sigma$
agents prefer almost any partner to solitude, as at $\mu = 2$, up to the change
of scale set by $\mu$. The benefit of commitment is therefore not an artefact of
the two distributions coinciding.

\subsection{Men, Women, and Other Partitions}

We did not distinguish men from women, as in the original marriage
problem~\cite{10.2307/2312726}, which might suggest we are dealing with stable
roommates instead. The distinction does not matter here. Partitioning the
society in two and matching only across the partition gives curves
indistinguishable from Figure~\ref{fig:utumt}: over four societies the two-sided
variant differs from the one-sided one by less than $0.03$, inside the
run-to-run spread.

Partitions of unequal size work too, with the matching becoming a random
injection from the smaller side into the larger and a random subset of the
larger sitting out each step. Further variants are available --- allowing a
subset of one partition to match within itself as well admits a reading as
bisexual agents. This flexibility contrasts with the matching problem, where
many results including Gale--Shapley require the two sides to be the same
size~\cite{roth1992two}.

\section{Who Gains and Who Loses}
\label{sec:who}

The equilibrium of Section~\ref{sec:nash} is symmetric because the society is
exchangeable: every agent faces the same distribution, so every agent has the
same best response. Real societies are not like that, and relaxing
exchangeability changes who the convention is for.

% >>> CHANGED FROM ORIGINAL. The original claimed, from a single run with ~50
% agents per group, that commitment benefits the less-liked more. At N = 10^4
% the column means are spread by only 1/sqrt(N) ~ 0.01 sigma, and over six
% replicates the two premia are statistically indistinguishable (+0.219 +/-
% 0.045 against +0.254 +/- 0.043). The claim is recovered, and strengthened,
% once genuine desirability heterogeneity is added. See ../../FINDINGS.md
% section 3 and ../../desirability-nash-equilibrium/liked_disliked.py.
It is tempting to look for the effect inside the model as it stands, by ranking
agents on the mean of their column of $A$ --- how much society likes them --- and
splitting off the top and bottom percentiles. This asks more of finite-size
effects than they can deliver. With $N = 10{,}000$ i.i.d.\ entries, column means
are spread by only $1/\sqrt{N} \approx 0.01\sigma$, so the ``$1\%$ most liked''
are more liked by about $0.03\sigma$, against a commitment benefit of
$0.25\sigma$. Over six societies the premium is $+0.219 \pm 0.045$ for the least
liked and $+0.254 \pm 0.043$ for the most liked: indistinguishable. Percentiles
exist there as luck of the draw, not as a property of an agent.

To ask the question properly the model needs genuine \emph{vertical}
heterogeneity. We add a per-agent desirability to every entry of that agent's
column,
%
\begin{align}
  A_{ij} = \varepsilon_{ij} + q_j,
  \quad
  \varepsilon_{ij} \sim \N(0, \sigma_\varepsilon),
  \quad
  q_j \sim \N(0, \sigma_q),
  \label{eq:quality}
\end{align}
%
holding $\sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \sigma_q^2 = 1$ so that runs at different
$\sigma_q$ stay comparable. Now some agents really are more widely liked, and
$\sigma_q$ tunes how much.

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig07_liked_disliked}
  \caption{Benefit of the convention $\Lambda = \sigma$ at $t = 100$ for the
  most and least liked $10\%$ of agents. Left: the exchangeable model,
  $\sigma_q = 0$, where the two are indistinguishable. Centre: with desirability
  spread $\sigma_q = 0.6$. Right: the benefit against $\sigma_q$, mean $\pm$
  standard error over six societies.}
  \label{fig:who}
  \Description{Three panels. The rightmost shows two curves: the least-liked
  benefit declining from positive towards zero, and the most-liked benefit
  crossing from positive to negative as sigma_q increases.}
\end{figure*}

  \caption{Benefit of the convention, $u_M - u$ at $t = 100$, by desirability
  spread, for the most and least liked $10\%$. Mean $\pm$ standard error over
  six societies.}
  \label{tab:who}
  \begin{tabular}{lcc}\toprule
    $\sigma_q$ & \textit{Least liked} $10\%$ & \textit{Most liked} $10\%$ \\ \midrule

    $0.2$ & $+0.249 \pm 0.014$ & $+0.108 \pm 0.013$ \\

Figure~\ref{fig:who} and Table~\ref{tab:who} give the result. The convention
helps the less desirable more, and past $\sigma_q \approx 0.4$ its effect on the
most desirable turns \emph{negative}: they are made strictly worse off by living
in a society that commits. The reading follows from
Section~\ref{sec:optimum}. Commitment is insurance against being left. Agents
whom everyone likes are rarely at risk of being left, so the insurance is worth
little to them, while the option value they give up --- the chance of meeting
someone better, which for them is a realistic prospect --- is worth a great deal.

This compounds the problem of Section~\ref{sec:nash} rather than relieving it.
There, a uniform society over-searches relative to its own optimum. Here, the
agents with the strongest private reason to defect from the convention are
precisely the ones it was not helping anyway, so enforcing it faces resistance
from exactly the quarter with the least to lose by resisting.

\section{Centralised Benchmarks}
\label{sec:benchmarks}

How far is any of this from what a planner could do on the same affinity matrix?
We use three reference points, computed by splitting the society into equal
halves and matching across them, which is the setting Gale--Shapley is defined
for: random pairing, the proposer-optimal stable matching~\cite{10.2307/2312726}
with average utility $u_{G\text{-}S}$, and the matching maximising total
surplus~\cite{10.2307/1831130, 10.2307/1829987},
%
\begin{align}
  u(\pi) = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^N \big(A_{i\pi(i)} + A_{\pi(i)i}\big).
  \label{eq:becker}
\end{align}

% >>> CHANGED FROM ORIGINAL (1). The original called the utilitarian optimum
% computationally impossible, counting N! matchings. It is a linear assignment
% problem, solvable exactly in O(N^3).
% >>> CHANGED FROM ORIGINAL (2). The original reported u_GS ~ 4 for the Gaussian
% model at N ~ 1000. We obtain 1.79. See ../../FINDINGS.md section 4.
Naively \eqref{eq:becker} ranges over $N!$ matchings, which for $N \approx 1000$
has $2568$ digits. But it is a linear assignment problem on the symmetrised
surplus $A_{ij} + A_{ji}$, so the Hungarian algorithm solves it exactly in
$O(N^3)$; we report it up to $N = 2000$.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig13_benchmarks}
  \caption{Left: aggregate utility at $t = 100$ for the dynamics against random
  pairing, the stable matching and the utilitarian optimum, as a function of
  $N$. Right: the two sides of the stable matching at $N = 1000$ do very
  different things.}
  \label{fig:bench}
  \Description{Left panel: five curves against N on a log axis, with the
  utilitarian optimum highest and random pairing flat near zero. Right panel:
  two overlaid histograms with clearly different means.}
\end{figure}

At $N = 1000$ in the Gaussian model the dynamics reach $1.213$ without
commitment and $1.429$ under the convention, the stable matching reaches
$u_{G\text{-}S} = 1.791$, and the utilitarian optimum $2.045$. Random pairing
gives $0.030$, that is $\mu$, as it must. The planner's margin grows with $N$,
since both centralised benchmarks have more to work with as the society grows
while the dynamics are limited by $T$.

The gap is a second, larger sense in which the decentralised market is
sub-optimal, on top of the $4.2\%$ of Section~\ref{sec:nash}. If affinities
could be inferred from manifest, correlated properties --- age, personality,
political leaning, education --- a central mechanism could propose a stable
matching and do considerably better than any convention.

The right panel adds a caveat about the size of that margin. A proposer-optimal
stable matching is far from symmetric between sides: at $N = 1000$ the proposing
side averages $2.454$ and the receiving side $1.128$, and $u_{G\text{-}S}$ is
the average of the two. The mean best-possible partner for a proposer is
$3.015$, so no matching of any kind places the two-sided average near it. Claims
about the planner's advantage should say which side of the market they mean.

\section{Empirical Comparison}
\label{sec:empirical}

Aggregate utility is hard to measure in a real society; the number of married
people is not. Figure~\ref{fig:mc} places the model's married share at each step
beside the share of men in England and Wales ever married by a given age, by
birth cohort~\cite{owidmarriagesanddivorces}.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig08_married_share}
  \caption{Left: share of married agents in the Gaussian model with
  $N = 10{,}000$, for $\Lambda = 1.5, 1, 0.5, 0, -2$ from the bottom line
  upwards. Right: share of men in England and Wales ever married by a given age,
  for the birth cohorts $1980, 1970, 1960, 1950, 1940$ from the bottom line
  upwards~\cite{owidmarriagesanddivorces}.}
  \label{fig:mc}
  \Description{Two panels of rising, saturating curves with similar shape; the
  model curves on the left and cohort data on the right.}
\end{figure}

The agreement is reasonable given how much the model ignores --- divorce, most
obviously --- and it was not built in: the resemblance is a dynamical
consequence of the matching process. Read through the model, the declining
married share across cohorts is a society-wide rise in $\Lambda$, which agrees
with rough expectations: marriage was once enforced by tradition and many
couples married without high affinity, and as those forces weakened people
became more demanding.

Section~\ref{sec:nash} suggests a reading of that drift which the data alone
does not supply. If a society's $\Lambda$ is maintained by convention, then
weakening the convention should move $\Lambda$ towards the value individual
agents prefer --- which is \emph{higher} than the social optimum, by $27\%$ in
our simulations. A secular rise in $\Lambda$ is what the model predicts a
society will do when it stops enforcing a threshold and lets agents choose,
and the prediction is that aggregate utility falls as it happens. We offer this
as an interpretation, not a finding: the model has no account of why conventions
weaken, and the comparison below is visual rather than fitted.

Two caveats bound how far Figure~\ref{fig:mc} can be pushed. Steps are not
years: the model's curve is concave from $t = 0$ while the data is S-shaped,
because nobody marries before their late teens, and any quantitative
cohort-to-$\Lambda$ mapping needs an explicit step-to-age map, which we do not
have. And the association of particular cohorts with particular $\Lambda$ values
is an eyeball match, not a fit.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\balance

The benefits of commitment disclosed here concern a narrow aspect of marriage,
and may be irrelevant once others are considered; Section~\ref{sec:who} shows
that even within the model the benefit is not uniform across agents.

The most salient simplification is that affinities are time-independent. Making
them time-dependent would bring divorce in for free: a married couple's affinity
may fall below $\Lambda$, at which point they unmarry and may break apart if one
finds another mate. It would also account for spontaneous break-ups, which
require only that affinity fall below one partner's utility of being single.
This is the extension most likely to connect the model to further data.

Three further limitations. The meeting process is uniform over the whole
society, whereas real meeting is strongly assortative by geography, education
and social network. Both $\Lopt$ and $\Leq$ are computed at the fixed horizon
$T = 100$, and we have not characterised how the gap between them varies with
$T$. And the equilibrium of Section~\ref{sec:nash} is located numerically, by
iterated best response over a grid; we have no closed-form characterisation of
$\Leq$, nor a proof that it is unique.

\section{Conclusions}

We studied an agent-based model of decentralised mate search in which marriage
is a commitment threshold $\Lambda$. As a uniform convention, $\Lambda$ has an
interior optimum: never committing and committing immediately are both worse
than committing at $\Lopt = 1.001\sigma$, one standard deviation above the mean
affinity. Commitment raises aggregate utility and lowers its dispersion at the
same time. This is the source of what sounds like good advice --- do not marry
the first person you like and do not search for the love of your life, but marry
if you like your partner more than a sigma above average.

The advice is not self-enforcing. Holding the society at $\Lambda = \sigma$, a
single agent does better by holding out for $1.312\sigma$, and iterated best
response settles at $\Leq = 1.27\sigma$, where everyone is $4.2\%$ worse off.
The cause is an externality: an agent that keeps searching and succeeds displaces
somebody, and that cost appears in the aggregate but not in its own payoff.
Agents optimising alone therefore over-search, and the society lands above the
threshold that would serve it best. Once agents differ in how much they are
liked, the convention's benefit concentrates on the least desirable and turns
negative for the most desirable, so the pressure against it comes from the
quarter with the least to lose.

The model leaves several directions open. A closed-form characterisation of
$\Leq$, and a proof of uniqueness, would put Section~\ref{sec:nash} on firmer
ground. The dependence of both $\Lopt$ and $\Leq$ on the horizon $T$ is
unexplored and is where the model makes contact with the observation that
different societies sustain different thresholds. Letting agents \emph{learn}
$\Lambda$ from their own history, rather than best-responding with full
knowledge, would test whether the equilibrium is reachable by agents with
realistic information --- Section~\ref{sec:empirical} sketches the rule such an
agent might use. And a time-dependent affinity would bring divorce, and with it
divorce rates, within reach of the same framework.
